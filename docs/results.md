- Results cover full HS trade volumes. They are not solar-only.
- Uncertain values are given as the median, with the 5th-95th percentile in brackets where it differs.

## 3. Lead-acid batteries: waste against recycling capacity (RQ4)

In 2025, lead-acid battery waste was 25,292 [23,806-26,088] t from 850720 and 34,038 t from starter batteries (850710). The sum was 59,330 [57,845-60,126] t. This is set against the documented nameplate capacity of 182,690 t/a of used-battery input (GIZ 2025). Capacity exceeds the trade-based waste by 123,360 t; the waste equals 32.5% of capacity. Capacity is nameplate, not throughput. No plant-level throughput figure was found.

In 2040 the sum is 0 t in series (a) and 24,676 t in series (b). *Why (a) declines:* it includes no imports after 2025.

**Documented explanations** for capacity exceeding the trade-based waste, as the sources state them; none is selected (`docs/phase3_results.md` §2.1):

1. Plants operate below nameplate capacity (GIZ 2025 pp.10, 34; BusinessDay 2017, 2018).
2. Some batteries are outside the trade series:
   - batteries manufactured in Nigeria (GIZ 2025 p.11; IDS 2016);
   - generation estimated from the vehicle fleet (Ogundele et al. 2020).
3. Used batteries flow in from neighbouring countries, reported as illegal (IDS 2016; Lead Battery Notes 2025; GIZ 2025 p.21).
4. Nigeria-reported and partner-reported trade records differ (§7).
5. Independent generation estimates are higher than the trade-based figures (GIZ 2025 p.9; EnviroNews 2017; BusinessDay 2018).
6. Used batteries are exported (BusinessDay 2017).

**Illustrative scenarios, not estimates.** If a stated share of 2025 lead-acid waste (850720 + 850710) went through informal recovery, the lead passing through it would be:

- 25% share: 9,270 t of lead.
- 50% share: 18,541 t of lead.
- 75% share: 27,811 t of lead.

## 7. Nigeria-reported and partner-reported imports (RQ1)

This section uses the wording verified in `docs/phase2_mirror_check.md` §4 (Amendment A6), with numbers recomputed from `data/processed/rq1_gap_usd_kg.csv`.

> Imports were taken from partner-reported trade (all reporters' exports to Nigeria; Source: UN Comtrade). Nigeria's own reported imports, which UN Comtrade holds for every year 2012-2025, differ from partner-reported values. Over 2012-2025 they are 61% lower in value and 45% lower in mass for lead-acid batteries (HS 850720), 75% lower in value and 80% lower in mass for lithium-ion batteries (HS 850760, 2015-2025), and 84% lower in value and 80% lower in mass for starter batteries (HS 850710). For static converters (HS 850440), Nigeria-reported values are 20% lower than partner-reported values, while Nigeria-reported mass is 3.5 times partner-reported mass. Nigeria's reported net weight for this code is flagged by UN Comtrade as estimated in every year from 2014. These differences are reported, not corrected. UN Comtrade lists differences in partner attribution, CIF and FOB valuation, timing, transit through third countries and classification among the general causes of such asymmetries.

> Before 2015, Nigeria reported in HS2007, which has no separate code for lithium-ion accumulators (850760). Nigeria-reported imports of "other accumulators" (HS 850780) were USD 30.2 m in 2012 and USD 23.5 m in 2013, against USD 4.2 m and 1.3 m reported by partners. These are not included in the study's series.

## 9. Limitations and what the study cannot claim

**Main limitations:**

- **Uncertainty:** the Monte Carlo uses 10,000 draws with a fixed seed. Only lead-acid and inverter lifetimes are sampled; other lifetimes are fixed values from published sources.
- **Battery and inverter lifetimes:** these use a Weibull shape k = 3.5, which has no source.
- **Panel lifetime formula:** the published IRENA form does not reproduce IRENA's stated supporting points. A calibrated alternative is a sensitivity (S23).
- **Sensitivities:** results are sensitive to the import source (Nigeria-reported vs partner-reported), to panel lifetime and to continuing imports (`docs/phase2_results.md` §3; `docs/phase3_results.md` §4).
- **Panel composition:** the IRENA composition shares sum to slightly more than one, because silver is given as less than 0.1%. This is reported, not corrected.

**What the study cannot claim** (plan §9, reproduced verbatim; section references in this list are to `docs/analysis_plan.md`):

1. **Health.** The study does not measure lead exposure or health outcomes. Ogijo and Ipetoro (P0 §5) are cited as documented context only.
2. **Unrecorded goods.** Trade data miss goods that are smuggled, undeclared or misdeclared, including informal flows through neighbouring countries.
3. **Routing.** Ember and GACC record the first destination (methodology v4 p.2). Goods routed through a third country, or re-exported from Nigeria, are mis-assigned.
4. **Domestic and second-hand goods.** Lead-acid batteries made in Nigeria (GIZ 2025 p.11), used imports, and products inside kits classified under other HS codes are not in the import series.
5. **Cells.** Cells assembled into modules in Nigeria are excluded from the main series (§2.6).
6. **Not solar-only.** Main results are full HS volumes. They are not solar-only. They include lead-acid batteries and inverters charged from the grid or generators, lithium-ion batteries in non-storage uses (850760), and chargers and power supplies (850440). The study does not claim any part of these volumes is attributable to solar unless a sourced share is shown as a secondary illustration (§3).
7. **Lifetimes [A2, A7].** These were not measured by the study. Phase 1b found published off-grid evidence for lead-acid batteries (GIZ 2018; ESMAP 2023), lithium-ion batteries (ESMAP 2023) and inverters (GIZ 2018), and these are the main values; S20 was therefore not applied. None of these is Nigerian field measurement. The only Nigerian lifetime study found (Ogundele et al. 2020) concerns vehicle starter batteries (850710).
8. **Waste fate.** End-of-life arising is not collection. The study does not say where waste goes, how much is collected, or how much is recycled soundly.
9. **Capacity.** Recycling capacity is nameplate. It is neither throughput nor a measure of environmental performance, and GIZ plant data are anonymised.
10. **Value.** Material value is gross contained value at international prices, not net revenue to anyone in Nigeria.
11. **Implementation.** For RQ6, the absence of a public report is not evidence that a requirement has not been implemented.
12. **Early years [A7].** Comtrade cohorts for 2012-2017 were retrieved in Phase 1b and are included for batteries and inverters. Equipment imported before 2012 is not included, and the panel series starts in 2017 (2012-2016 panels appear only in sensitivity S21). Waste arising in the early years from older equipment is therefore not counted.
13. **Locked-in waste.** The main series is waste from equipment imported to date. It is not a forecast of total future waste. S10 is a sensitivity, not a forecast.
14. **Panel MW.** Panel capacity is an Ember estimate derived from price, not a measured quantity.
15. **Project Volt.** The comparison with Project Volt is limited to its public headline figure and is not like for like (§5). The study does not assess Project Volt's methods or attribute differences to its internal assumptions.
16. **Inverter and lithium-ion material value [A3].** Inverters carry no material value. Lithium-ion value covers copper and aluminium only, from an industry-commissioned automotive source.
