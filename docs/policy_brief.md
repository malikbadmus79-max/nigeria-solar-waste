# The solar boom's hidden bill

Nigeria's coming wave of solar panel and battery waste. Policy brief, November 2026. Malik Kolawole Badmus.

Full results, data and code: [github.com/malikbadmus79-max/nigeria-solar-waste](https://github.com/malikbadmus79-max/nigeria-solar-waste) (`docs/results.md`). Trade data: Source: UN Comtrade. Ember and World Bank data: CC BY 4.0.

## Summary

Nigeria is importing solar and backup-power equipment in record volumes. Panel imports rose from 5,484 tonnes (an estimated 73 MW) in 2017 to 101,528 tonnes (1,904 MW) in 2025, and reached 138,772 tonnes in the first nine months of 2026. Every panel, battery and inverter becomes waste, and batteries go first. This study estimates the waste already locked in by equipment imported to date, sets it against documented recycling capacity, and reviews what the 2024 Battery Control Regulations require against what is publicly reported.

- Lead-acid batteries are today's waste stream. In 2025, used lead-acid batteries came to an estimated 59,330 tonnes: 25,292 tonnes of deep-cycle and other lead-acid batteries and 34,038 tonnes of starter batteries.
- Capacity is not the constraint for lead. That waste equals 32.5% of the documented nameplate capacity of licensed recyclers (182,690 tonnes a year). The question is where used batteries go, not whether plants exist to take them.
- Lithium-ion is tomorrow's. Lithium-ion imports overtook lead-acid by value in 2022 and by mass in 2024; in 2025 they were about three times as large by mass. If imports continue at the 2025 level, lithium-ion battery waste reaches about 40,000 tonnes a year by 2040, and no recycling capacity for lithium-ion, solar panels or inverters is documented in Nigeria.
- The boom is recent. Imports from 2022 to 2026 alone add 176,676 to 234,588 tonnes of panel and battery waste by 2040.

Batteries are lead-acid (HS 850720) and lithium-ion (HS 850760); starter batteries are counted separately. The first row falls after 2025 only because it includes no future purchases; the second row is a scenario, not a forecast. Panels last decades: most panels imported to date (352,016 to 420,659 tonnes) are still in use in 2040.

The value of what is thrown away. Waste from equipment imported to date contains metals worth USD 471 to 575 million at current prices (USD 900 million to 1.08 billion if imports continue), before collection and processing costs. Lead-acid batteries alone contain 170,206 tonnes of lead. Formal recovery (90%) would return about 153,000 tonnes of it; informal smelting recovers only 50-60% (GIZ 2025).

Where the lead goes matters. If a quarter of 2025's used lead-acid batteries went through informal recovery, about 9,300 tonnes of lead would pass through it; at half, about 18,500 tonnes. These are illustrations, not estimates: no public data show the actual share.

## The rules exist; the public record is thin

The National Environmental (Battery Control) Regulations, S.I. No. 11 of 2024, require producers to register for extended producer responsibility, set up collection, fund a producer responsibility organisation, report annually, and test recycling workers for lead twice a year. Of 17 duties reviewed (including PV panels under the e-waste regulations, S.I. No. 79 of 2022):

| Public record, March 2024 to November 2026 | Duties |
|---|---|
| Implementation found | 3 |
| Statement of intent only | 3 |
| Record indicates not implemented | 3 |
| No public record found | 8 |

Blood-lead testing of workers is reported as not conducted in the industry (GIZ, May 2025). In late November 2025, NESREA sealed recycling facilities in Ogijo, Ogun State; the public record of that action cites the absence of blood-lead testing, improper battery breaking and slag sold for landfilling and construction at those facilities. No public registration figures, collection volumes, fee schedules or annual producer reports were found. Absence of a public record is not evidence of non-compliance, but it means the public cannot see whether the system works.

Trade records also disagree. Nigeria's own reported imports are 61% lower in value than partner-reported figures for lead-acid batteries and 84% lower for starter batteries (2012-2025), and 75% lower for lithium-ion batteries (2015-2025). Such gaps can arise from transit trade, classification, timing, valuation, or smuggled and undeclared goods; the data do not show which. The study uses partner-reported figures.

## Recommendations

1. NESREA: publish the extended producer responsibility register, the producer responsibility organisation's collection and recycling volumes, and annual producer reports. These are already required; publishing them would show whether used batteries reach licensed plants.
2. NESREA, Ogun and Lagos State environmental agencies: enforce blood-lead testing and environmentally sound battery breaking at every licensed plant, and act on informal smelting, where the health risk concentrates.
3. Federal Ministry of Environment and NESREA: extend take-back and collection to lithium-ion batteries and solar panels now, before lithium-ion waste grows from hundreds to tens of thousands of tonnes a year.
4. Rural Electrification Agency: make battery take-back a condition of every public solar project, as Regulation 11(2) already requires of contractors.
5. Standards Organisation of Nigeria: enforce minimum quality and life standards for batteries, inverters and panels. Products that fail early add to waste.
6. Nigeria Customs Service and the National Bureau of Statistics: reconcile battery and solar import records with partner data and publish product-level detail, so that planners can track the inflow.
7. Investors and recyclers: lithium-ion, panel and inverter recycling is a gap with a known, growing feedstock and recoverable value.

## Limits

These are estimates from trade data, not measurements of waste collected. Volumes are full trade categories, not solar-only: they include batteries and inverters charged from the grid or generators and, for inverters, chargers and power supplies. Lifetimes come from published off-grid studies, not Nigerian field data. Recycling capacity is nameplate, not throughput. The study does not measure lead exposure; health cases are cited as documented context.
